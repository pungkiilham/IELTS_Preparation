**Reading Passage: The Neuroscience of Memory Formation**

**A** Memory is one of the most complex and remarkable functions of the human brain. Far from being a single system, memory comprises multiple interconnected processes that allow us to encode, store, and retrieve information. Traditionally, neuroscientists have categorised memory into two broad types: explicit (declarative) memory, which involves conscious recall of facts and events, and implicit (non-declarative) memory, which encompasses skills, habits, and conditioned responses. This distinction, first proposed by Canadian psychologist Endel Tulving in 1972, remains fundamental to our understanding of how the brain handles past experiences.

**B** The process of memory formation begins with encoding — the transformation of sensory input into a neural representation that the brain can store. Attention plays a critical role at this stage; information that is not attended to is rapidly lost. Research by cognitive psychologist Linda Hasher at the University of Toronto has demonstrated that older adults often experience memory difficulties not because they cannot store information, but because their attentional filters become less efficient, allowing irrelevant information to compete for encoding resources. This finding challenges the common assumption that memory decline in ageing is primarily a storage problem.

**C** Once encoded, information enters short-term or working memory, a temporary storage system with limited capacity. The classic research of George Miller in 1956 suggested that the average adult can hold approximately seven items in working memory at any one time. However, more recent studies indicate that the true capacity may be closer to three to five items when distractions are present and that individual variation is considerable. Working memory serves as a cognitive workspace where information is manipulated and prepared for long-term storage or discarded.

**D** The transfer of information from short-term to long-term memory is known as consolidation, a process that occurs primarily during sleep. Contrary to popular belief, consolidation is not a single event but an ongoing process that can continue for years. The hippocampus, a seahorse-shaped structure deep within the temporal lobe, is essential for this process. Patients with damage to the hippocampus, such as the famous case of Henry Molaison (known as HM), can form new short-term memories but cannot transfer them into long-term storage. However, their ability to learn new motor skills — such as drawing while looking in a mirror — remains intact, providing strong evidence for the separation of explicit and implicit memory systems.

**E** Every time a long-term memory is retrieved, it becomes temporarily malleable and must be reconsolidated. This reconsolidation process is thought to be the mechanism by which memories are updated and modified. It also explains why eyewitness testimony is notoriously unreliable: the act of recalling an event can alter the original memory, particularly when leading questions are asked. Psychologist Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated in her landmark studies that entirely false memories could be implanted in subjects simply through the phrasing of questions. Subsequent research has confirmed that approximately 30% of subjects can be led to develop detailed false memories of events that never occurred.

**F** Emotion exerts a powerful influence on memory formation. The amygdala, an almond-shaped structure adjacent to the hippocampus, modulates the consolidation of emotional memories. This explains why people typically remember emotionally charged events — such as where they were during a major disaster — with vivid detail, even decades later. However, these so-called 'flashbulb memories' are not as accurate as individuals believe them to be. Studies comparing participants' recollections of the 9/11 attacks taken shortly after the event with recollections taken one year later revealed significant discrepancies, despite participants expressing high confidence in their memories.

**G** Understanding the mechanisms of memory has significant practical applications. Techniques such as spaced repetition and elaborative encoding, both grounded in neuroscience research, have been shown to dramatically improve long-term retention. Spaced repetition involves reviewing information at gradually increasing intervals, exploiting the brain's consolidation processes. Elaborative encoding, by contrast, involves connecting new information to existing knowledge, creating richer neural networks that facilitate later retrieval. These evidence-based strategies offer substantial benefits for students and lifelong learners seeking to optimise their study practices.
