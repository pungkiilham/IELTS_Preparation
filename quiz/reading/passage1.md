## Passage 1: The Psychology of Color
*Medium difficulty | 14 minutes*

**Color psychology is the study of how colors affect human behavior, mood, and cognitive processes. While cultural associations with colors vary significantly across different societies, certain emotional responses to colors appear to be universal. Research conducted by psychologist Angela Wickham at the University of London found that warm colors such as red and orange tend to increase heart rate and create feelings of excitement, while cool colors like blue and green promote calmness and reduce stress.**

**The corporate world has embraced these findings extensively. Major companies invest millions in color analysis to determine the most effective branding for their products. Blue, often associated with trust and reliability, dominates the financial sector, while food companies frequently utilize red and yellow to stimulate appetite and create energy. However, experts caution that color alone cannot drive purchasing decisions; it must work in conjunction with other design elements.**

**The impact of color extends beyond marketing into therapeutic applications. Color therapy, an alternative healing practice, uses colored light to treat various physical and emotional conditions. Blue light, for instance, is commonly used to treat seasonal affective disorder, while green environments have been shown to accelerate recovery times in hospital settings. Despite the growing popularity of these applications, scientific consensus remains cautious, with researchers calling for more rigorous controlled studies.**

## Passage 2: The Decline of Insects
*Hard difficulty | 14 minutes*

**Entomologists worldwide are sounding the alarm about the accelerating decline of insect populations. A comprehensive study published in Biological Conservation revealed that more than 40% of insect species are currently threatened with extinction, with annual population declines averaging 2.5% over the past three decades. This trajectory, if unchecked, could lead to the collapse of ecosystems within the next century.**

**The causes of this decline are multifaceted. Agricultural intensification has resulted in massive habitat loss, with monoculture farming eliminating the biodiversity that insects depend upon. Pesticide use, particularly neonicotinoids, has been directly linked to colony collapse disorder in bees and has devastated beneficial insect populations. Additionally, climate change has disrupted seasonal patterns, throwing off the delicate synchronization between insects and their food sources.**

**The ecological implications extend far beyond the insects themselves. As primary pollinators, insects support approximately 75% of global food crops. Their decline threatens agricultural productivity and food security on a global scale. Furthermore, insects form the base of many food webs; their disappearance would cascade through ecosystems, affecting birds, mammals, and aquatic species that depend on them for sustenance.**

**Conservation efforts have begun, albeit insufficiently. The European Union has banned neonicotinoids for outdoor use, and several nations have implemented pollinator protection strategies. However, experts argue that these measures represent merely palliative solutions. Fundamental changes to agricultural practices, including the widespread adoption of agroecological methods, will be necessary to reverse these trends. The window for action, researchers warn, is rapidly closing.**

## Passage 3: Artificial Intelligence in Education
*Hard difficulty | 20 minutes*

**The integration of artificial intelligence into educational settings represents one of the most significant technological transformations in modern pedagogy. Unlike earlier iterations of educational technology, which primarily served as supplementary tools, AI systems now increasingly serve as primary instructional agents. Adaptive learning platforms, powered by sophisticated machine learning algorithms, can personalize curriculum delivery to match individual student learning patterns and pace.**

**Proponents argue that this technology addresses longstanding inequities in education. Students in under-resourced schools can access high-quality, personalized instruction that was previously available only to wealthier students. AI-powered language learning applications have demonstrated particular effectiveness, with some systems achieving proficiency levels comparable to human tutors at a fraction of the cost. Furthermore, these systems can provide immediate feedback, allowing students to correct misconceptions before they become entrenched.**

**However, critics raise substantial concerns. The irreplaceable nature of human teachers in fostering social-emotional development cannot be overstated. Students learn not merely academic content but social cues, empathy, and collaborative skills through human interaction. Additionally, algorithmic bias, reflecting the historical data on which AI systems are trained, can perpetuate existing inequalities rather than eliminating them. Privacy concerns regarding the extensive data collection required for personalized learning also warrant serious consideration.**

**The most effective approach likely involves thoughtful integration rather than wholesale replacement. The most successful implementations position AI as a tool to enhance human teachers rather than replace them, handling routine content delivery while freeing educators to focus on mentoring, project-based learning, and the uniquely human aspects of education that machines cannot replicate. This hybrid model, combining technological efficiency with human insight, offers the most promising path forward for 21st-century education.**

## Passage 4: Urban Planning
*Medium difficulty | 14 minutes*

**Urban planning plays a crucial role in shaping the cities we live in. Modern urban planners must balance competing interests: economic development, environmental sustainability, and quality of life for residents. One of the most significant challenges facing cities today is housing affordability. As populations grow and cities expand, the gap between housing supply and demand continues to widen.**

**Sustainable urban development has become a priority for many city governments. Green building practices, renewable energy integration, and efficient public transportation systems are key components of environmentally conscious city planning. Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) is a popular approach that concentrates housing, jobs, and services around public transit hubs, reducing the need for private vehicles.**

**The social dimension of urban planning cannot be overlooked. Access to green spaces, recreational facilities, and community centers contributes significantly to residents' well-being. Research has shown that well-designed public spaces encourage social interaction and strengthen community bonds. However, urban regeneration projects can sometimes displace long-term residents, raising questions about gentrification and social justice.**

**The future of urban planning will likely be shaped by technological innovations. Smart city initiatives use data and connectivity to improve municipal services, from traffic management to energy efficiency. While these technologies offer tremendous potential, they also raise concerns about surveillance and digital privacy.**

## Passage 5: The Science of Sleep
*Medium difficulty | 14 minutes*

**Sleep is essential for physical and mental health, yet millions of people worldwide suffer from sleep disorders. The average adult requires seven to nine hours of sleep per night for optimal functioning. However, modern lifestyles, with artificial lighting, screens, and demanding work schedules, have significantly disrupted natural sleep patterns.**

**During sleep, the body undergoes crucial restorative processes. The brain consolidates memories, processing and storing information learned during the day. Growth hormone is released, facilitating tissue repair and muscle growth. The immune system is strengthened, making individuals more resistant to infections.**

**Insomnia, the most common sleep disorder, is characterized by difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early. Chronic insomnia can lead to serious health problems including obesity, cardiovascular disease, and depression. Treatment options range from lifestyle changes and cognitive behavioral therapy to medication.**

**Melatonin, often called the "sleep hormone," plays a key role in regulating the sleep-wake cycle. Light exposure, particularly blue light from screens, suppresses melatonin production and can delay sleep onset. Healthcare professionals recommend limiting screen time before bed and maintaining a consistent sleep schedule to improve sleep quality.**
